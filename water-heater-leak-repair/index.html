Where is the water coming from? That question decides whether a water heater leak costs a little or a lot. Relief valves weep when they wear out or when household pressure runs high. Drain valves, especially the plastic ones found on many factory tanks, start dripping as they age. Fittings on top of the tank corrode where copper meets steel, and flexible connectors can crack or lose their seal. Condensation can mimic a leak for weeks on a gas unit or in a humid space. And sometimes the tank itself has rusted through. Across Royal Oak, MI, we sort out which one you have before recommending a thing.

Our diagnosis is methodical. We dry the area, check the relief valve outlet and discharge pipe, inspect the drain valve, and follow the hot and cold lines up to the fittings on top of the tank. On gas units, we check the combustion chamber for water, which can point to condensation or, more seriously, a failing tank. If the relief valve has been discharging, we put a gauge on the system to read static pressure and check for thermal expansion, since a closed plumbing system without an expansion tank can push pressure high enough to open the valve. After all of that do we recommend a fix.

Once we know the source, the repair is usually straightforward. A new T&P valve, a brass drain valve in place of a weeping plastic one, fresh dielectric fittings or supply connectors, and an expansion tank or pressure-reducing valve where pressure is to blame can all be handled on the spot. A corroded tank is different. No sealant or patch will hold, so we explain replacement honestly and walk through the choices. When to Call for Water Heater Leak Repair

Most tanks announce trouble weeks or months ahead with rumbling noises, rusty water, or hot showers that keep getting shorter. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Moisture or rust flakes under the burner access cover
  • Rusty or discolored hot water paired with dampness at the base
  • Water dripping from the relief valve or out of the end of its discharge pipe
  • Steady drips from the drain valve near the base
  • A water bill that rises without any change in usage
  • Water collecting under or beside the heater
  • A musty odor or soft floor around the heater location
  • Rust, green corrosion, or white crust on the fittings at the top of the tank

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Reading a water heater leak correctly is harder than it looks. A drip that appears to come from the bottom may start at a fitting on top and run down the jacket, and condensation on a cold tank can pass for a failing tank. Replacing the wrong part wastes money, and missing a failing tank can lead to a much bigger flood later. Safety is part of the picture as well. The relief valve protects against dangerous temperature and pressure, and capping or plugging it to stop a drip can set up a violent failure. Tightening corroded fittings on an old tank can crack them, and electric models run on 240-volt power that do not mix well with wet floors.

We work through leaks step by step and fix the cause along with the drip. We start by drying the unit, watch for fresh water, put a gauge on the system whenever the relief valve is involved, and check for thermal expansion problems that make valves weep. We replace valves and fittings with durable replacements, swap plastic drain valves for brass, and add an expansion tank or pressure-reducing valve when pressure is the root problem. When the tank is the problem, we say so directly and explain replacement options, including the drain pan and leak protection that can keep the next failure from soaking your floors.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • Draining sediment every year helps the bottom of the tank last longer
  • Make a habit of checking around the heater for moisture or corrosion every few weeks
  • A battery leak alarm beside the heater costs little and can catch a problem days sooner
  • An expansion tank that is present and properly charged keeps the relief valve from opening every cycle
  • Inspecting the anode rod periodically is one of the best ways to delay a tank leak
  • A properly drained pan under the tank catches small leaks before they reach your floors
  • Have household pressure measured, since high pressure wears out relief valves and fittings faster
