A gas water heater is a fairly simple machine, which is part of why it lasts. A burner under the tank heats the water, exhaust rises through a flue that runs up the center of the tank, and a gas control valve on the front acts as both thermostat and safety shutoff. That valve only lets gas reach the burner while it senses a flame, through a thermocouple on standing-pilot models or a flame sensor on electronic ignition models. When something goes wrong, it is usually at one of those points. In Royal Oak, MI, we regularly find thermocouples that no longer hold the valve open, pilots starved by a lint-clogged flame arrestor screen, gas valves that have failed internally, and flue or vent problems that let exhaust spill back into the room.

Diagnosing a gas water heater properly means checking combustion, not just parts. Across Royal Oak, our technicians measure thermocouple output in millivolts, study the pilot and burner flame for color and shape, read gas pressure at the valve, and test draft at the hood so we know exhaust is leaving the house. We check the combustion chamber for soot and scorching, which point to a blocked flue or poor combustion air, and we test for carbon monoxide near the heater. Safety always comes first: a gas smell means leaving the house and calling the gas utility from outside before anything else, and Royal Oak Water Heater can take over from there.

Most gas water heater repairs are finished in a single visit. We replace thermocouples, flame sensors, pilot assemblies, and igniters, clean burners and flame arrestor screens, swap failed gas control valves, and correct vent connectors that have separated or lost their slope. We also flush sediment, the cause of that popping or rumbling sound and a major reason tanks overheat at the bottom and fail early. When the tank itself is leaking or the heater is deep into its service life, we tell you honestly and walk you through replacement. New installations are sized by first-hour rating, not just gallons, and include the right venting, a sediment trap on the gas line, an expansion tank where needed, and seismic straps and a drain pan where code requires them. Warning Signs to Watch For

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • A pilot light that will not stay lit
  • Leaks at the drain valve, the fittings, or the bottom of the tank
  • Signs of backdrafting, such as soot, condensation, or an exhaust smell at the vent
  • A yellow, lazy, or sooty burner flame instead of a crisp blue one
  • A cold tank and a burner that will not come on
  • Showers that go cold sooner than they did a year ago
  • A tank that pops and bangs while it heats, a classic sign of sediment
  • Any smell of gas around the appliance, which calls for leaving and phoning the gas utility

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Consider the risks first. Fuel piping, an open flame, and exhaust laced with carbon monoxide come with every gas water heater. A loose flare or threaded joint can leak gas, a vent that is disconnected or poorly sloped can spill exhaust back into the room, and a gas valve installed without a leak test is a risk you cannot see. Then there is the diagnostic side: a pilot that goes out can mean a weak thermocouple, a dirty pilot orifice, a clogged flame arrestor, a draft problem, or a failing gas valve, and replacing them one by one is slow and costly.

Prevention Tips That Actually Help

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Give the heater breathing room and keep flammables well away from it
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years and replaced when it is worn
  • Flush the tank on a regular schedule so sediment never bakes onto the bottom
  • Make sure the vent pipe stays connected, sloped upward, and free of rust
  • Install carbon monoxide alarms on every level of the home and test them monthly
  • Vacuum lint and dust from the flame arrestor screen at the base a few times a year
